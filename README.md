## About the Project

${\color{#ff0000}S}{\color{#ff6e00}M}{\color{#ffdd00}L}$ *(**Smart Music Lamp**)* is a project that started with the idea of creating a Wi-Fi controlled lamp and ended up becoming much more than that. It began with the intention of making a personal version of a Sonoff, and the ESP32 was chosen because its power was more than enough for the task.

Thinking it over, it was a shame to waste so much power on a simple task of turning a lamp on and off.

So what should I add to truly leverage the ESP32 and end up with a more complete and functional product?

Watching my wife cook while listening to music on her phone, which she would leave anywhere, the idea came to me: give the lamp an audio system *(Bluetooth speaker)*. I told her, wouldn't it be great to cook while listening to music without having to leave your phone somewhere random? You'll be able to control the lamp and stream music from your phone via Bluetooth to the lamp, having music in the kitchen.

If I already have a lamp that plays music, why not make it more cheerful and add colors?

To do this, I added a **Neopixel** strip that is also controlled via the web from your phone, offering lighting effects including effects that sync with the music the lamp is playing.

Still not satisfied, I added a temperature and humidity sensor to display that data on the web interface embedded in the ESP32.

Since I live in a country where power outages are very common, and I had the ability to play music through the lamp, why not take advantage of it?

I implemented a dual power supply system *(mains and battery)* that allows you to keep controlling the lamp via the web and listen to music after a power outage. Of course, I couldn't use the **Neopixels** since they consume too much power.

### Goals

- **_Control the lamp on/off from your mobile phone._**
- **_Play music through the lamp via Bluetooth from your mobile phone._**
- **_Control the Neopixel strip for different lighting effects._**
- **_Monitor battery charge and usage._**
- **_Monitor temperature and humidity._**
- **_Web-based control of the Bluetooth speaker's physical buttons._**
- **_Embedded web interface for real-time control from any mobile device._**
- **_Multi-device control: multiple phones connected at the same time, one as master and the rest as slaves._**
- **_Defined behavior on power loss, WiFi disconnection, and low battery._**

### Components

The ESP32 was chosen for this project because of its power, number of analog inputs, and Wi-Fi connectivity. It features dual power supply, using a 5V power source and a 3.7V battery. A protected charger handles battery charging, and a DC-DC step-up converter boosts the battery voltage to 5V to power the ESP32 and all modules except the Neopixel strip, which is always powered from the mains supply.

A dual relay module controls the LED lamp and the Bluetooth module. A DHT22 sensor measures temperature and humidity. Additional components implement supporting circuits.

### Diagram

The following schematic illustrates the power connections for the project.

![smlpower](https://github.com/alexminator/SML/blob/master/img/SML_power.png)

> **Note**: 
> The output voltage of the lamp's power supply that feeds the high-brightness white LEDs is not the one shown in the schematic; it is much higher. It is not important to know it for this project.

> ### :point_right: You can find the schematic [here](https://github.com/alexminator/SML/blob/master/img/SML_power.fzz). :star:

For a better understanding of the dual power supply **(load sharing)** used, here is the following diagram.

![loadsharing](https://github.com/alexminator/SML/blob/master/img/load-sharing.png)

When 5V power is applied, this circuit turns off the MOSFET and stops current flow from the battery to the load (DC-DC module), effectively disconnecting the battery. As long as the ${\color{#ffdd00}input\ voltage\ minus\ the\ Schottky\ diode\ drop}$ is above the ${\color{#ffdd00}battery\ voltage\ minus\ the\ drain-source\ voltage\ drop}$, the load draws power from the 5V supply through the Schottky diode. This allows the battery to charge normally without external disturbances.

The chosen MOSFET must have the lowest possible RDS(on) to minimize power loss, must handle the current your circuit draws from the battery, and must have a VGS(th) between 0V and -2.4V.

The diode prevents current from flowing from the battery back to the 5V power supply. It must be a Schottky diode that can handle the maximum current draw of the loads. **The RG resistor (10K)** ensures the MOSFET turns on and connects the battery to the load when the 5V power supply is removed.

The DC-DC boost converter must be adjusted so its output voltage is 5V whether powered from mains or battery.

To monitor the battery voltage, place a resistive voltage divider at the output of the TP4056 charger module and connect its output to pin D33 of the ESP32.

![battmon](https://github.com/alexminator/SML/blob/master/img/battmon.png)

With the excellent library by [danilopinotti/Battery18650Stats](https://github.com/danilopinotti/Battery18650Stats), the battery voltage is read and expressed as a percentage. Since batteries in use don't always reach the ideal 4.2V maximum, **danilopinotti's** library didn't give practical values. So I modified the library to include configurable maximum and minimum voltage values in its parameters for more accurate percentage readings.

The TP4056 module has 2 indicator LEDs — one to indicate charging ${\color{#ff0000}(red)}$ and another to indicate the battery is fully charged ${\color{#0022ff}(blue)}$. Analyzing the module's diagram, these LEDs connect to pins 6 **(standby)** and 7 **(charge)** of the TP4056 IC. To activate the LEDs, these pins output a logic low, which we detect on our ESP32 to know the charger status. To detect a logic low, implement the following circuit.

![zero](https://github.com/alexminator/SML/blob/master/img/zero-detect.png)

As shown in the diagram, if pins 6 or 7 output more than 0V, the LEDs are off and diode (D17) does not conduct, so 3.3V reaches the ESP32 input. When the module is charging or the battery is fully charged, there will be a logic low on those pins. The corresponding LED turns on and diode (D17) conducts, reflecting a low level on the ESP32 pin. In code, this is represented by the variables ${\color{#ffdd00}isCharging}$ and ${\color{#ffdd00}fullyCharge}$.

During testing I faced another challenge: when the Bluetooth speaker is turned on, the volume is always at half. This seems to be normal behavior for these Bluetooth speakers, so to control the volume we need to use the physical buttons. But since the lamp will be in an inaccessible location, we can't. And even if we turn up our phone volume to max, we may not achieve the desired level. So how can we remotely control physical buttons?

Analyzing the schematic of some common Bluetooth speakers on the market (Chinese ones), I noticed the buttons switch to ground (GND) on an IC pin. This makes it easy to emulate a button using the ESP32. The following diagram shows the solution.

![bt](https://github.com/alexminator/SML/blob/master/img/BT_conn.png)

The solution basically uses MOSFETs as simple switches — when activated, they ground the Bluetooth IC pin. In this Bluetooth speaker model, each button has dual functions depending on the press duration, which is also emulated in code. We only need to control 3 buttons: **Volume+(FF)**, **Volume-(REW)**, and **Play(Pause)**. By controlling the pulse duration to the MOSFET gate in code, we simulate the button press duration.

The diagram also shows how to sample audio from the module's audio output. A voltage divider circuit is implemented, and the signal passes through a resistor and electrolytic capacitor to pin **D36(VP)**.

As seen in the power connection diagram earlier, turning the Bluetooth module on and off is done through a relay **(IN2)** controlled by the ESP32 from **pin D18**.

Finally, the DHT22 temperature and humidity sensor connects to pin **D23**. This sensor can be powered with 5V or 3V, so there's no issue choosing either. For better temperature and humidity monitoring, it is recommended that the sensor be exposed to the outside air.

## Code

The project essentially boils down to controlling the lamp via the web from any connected device. The ESP32 stays in station mode, waiting for another device to enter AP (portable hotspot) mode with the correct credentials to connect. Why did I choose station mode?

On the web interface, you'll notice Wi-Fi signal strength indicators in the bottom right corner showing the connection quality between your device and the lamp. This can only be achieved if the ESP32 is in station mode.

The ESP32 creates an asynchronous server (**[ESPAsyncWebServer](https://github.com/me-no-dev/ESPAsyncWebServer.git)**), meaning it can handle multiple clients simultaneously **(8)** and sends data in JSON format via WebSocket. With this setup, up to 8 devices connected to the lamp receive real-time updates, and changes are reflected on all devices simultaneously.

The ESP32 hosts an embedded website in **LittleFS** (the project builds a LittleFS image, not SPIFFS); the web content is in the project's ${\color{#ffdd00}data}$ folder. The code is heavily commented, so here's a summary.

Three objects are created:
  * `StripLed` — Neopixel strip control
  * `Led` — ESP32 onboard LED control
  * `Battery` — Battery control and monitoring

The remaining libraries handle lighting effects and music-synchronized effects **(VU)**, along with the web server, battery monitoring, and DHT22 sensor. Below are the most important variables you may need to adjust.

1. Debug variables — all of them live in `src/config/debug_config.h`
  * `DEBUGLEVEL` — verbosity: **0** none, **1** errors, **2** +warnings, **3** +debug (current default), **4** +verbose
  * Category switches, enabled by uncommenting the line: `DEBUG_SYSTEM`, `DEBUG_WIFI`, `DEBUG_WEB`, `DEBUG_WEBSOCKET`, `DEBUG_LED`, `DEBUG_BATTERY`, `DEBUG_TEMPERATURE`, `DEBUG_NETWORK`, `DEBUG_BLUETOOTH`, `DEBUG_POWER_MANAGEMENT`
  * For a release build you do not need to edit the file either: add `-DDEBUGLEVEL=0` to `build_flags` in `platformio.ini`. Measured on this project that drops flash from 1,085,005 B to 1,058,845 B (−26 KB) and RAM from 58,456 B to 57,648 B.

> **Note**:
> At `DEBUGLEVEL` **0** every log line and its string literals are compiled out. That is the cheapest 26 KB you will ever save.

2. Neopixel strip variables
  * `#define N_PIXELS` — number of LEDs in your strip
  * `int brightness` — initial brightness (default 130, a bit over half brightness)

3. Battery management variables
  * `#define MAXV` — Maximum voltage when battery is fully charged
  * `#define MINV` — Minimum voltage to start charging (default 3.2V)

 > **Note**:
 > For accuracy, charge the battery using the TP4056 module. When the full charge LED turns on, measure the battery voltage and use that as the maximum value. For the minimum value, the DC-DC step-up module specifications state that to achieve 5V output, the input must be at least 2V. However, 3.2V is recommended for safety.

4. Web / timing variables — in `src/config/config.h`
  * `WEBSOCKET_UPDATE_INTERVAL` — full-state broadcast to every client (3 s)
  * `WEBSOCKET_SENSOR_INTERVAL` / `SENSOR_CHECK_INTERVAL` — battery and sensor broadcasts (3 s / 5 s)
  * `BATTERY_CHECK_INTERVAL` — how often the battery is sampled (3 s)
  * `WIFI_LOST_STRIP_TIMEOUT_DEFAULT` — default WiFi-loss timeout before the strip turns off (30 s, overridable from the web)

> **Note**:
> The project currently uses [PlatformIO](https://platformio.org/) as its development environment. The `platformio.ini` file in the project root contains all build configuration, dependencies, and serial port settings.

## Project Structure

```
SML/
├── src/
│   ├── main.cpp              boot: GPIO, FastLED, LittleFS, state, WiFi, web, tasks
│   ├── config/               config.h (timings, limits), pins.h, debug.h (log engine),
│   │                         debug_config.h (verbosity), secrets.h.example
│   ├── effects/              one header per effect + Effect.h base class,
│   │                         EffectRegistry.cpp/.h (single table), PaletteManager.*,
│   │                         effects.h (aggregator)
│   ├── vu/                   VU effects: VUEffect.h, vu1..vu9, vu.h (aggregator)
│   ├── net/                  WebServer.cpp (HTTP + WiFi + LittleFS + mDNS),
│   │                         WebSocket.cpp (protocol), data.cpp/.h (WiFi constants)
│   ├── power/                PowerMgr.cpp — power state machine
│   ├── state/                AppState.cpp/.h — global state, mutexes, persistence
│   └── tasks/                tasks.cpp — the six FreeRTOS tasks
├── data/                     web app served by the ESP32 (HTML, CSS, JS, fonts, images)
├── docs/                     design notes and reviews (git-ignored)
├── test/
└── platformio.ini            build configuration and pinned dependencies
```

The six tasks are `TaskWebSocket` (cleanup + broadcasts), `TaskBatteryMonitor` (sampling + power state machine), `TaskLEDControl` (effect render at ~50 Hz and the Peek snapshot), `TaskWiFiMonitor` (link state, mDNS re-registration, strip suspension), `TaskSensor` (DHT22) and `TaskOnboardLED` (status blink pattern).

**Adding an effect.** Create a subclass of `Effect` in `src/effects/`, implement `render()`, add **one line** to `effectRegistry[]` in `src/effects/EffectRegistry.cpp` and add the card in the front-end. The effect ID *is* its position in the table: appending is safe, but inserting or removing a line shifts every later ID and invalidates the `effectId` saved in `/state.json` and the front-end map.

**WiFi credentials are not committed.** Put `WIFI_SSID` and `WIFI_PASS` in a `.env` file in the project root — `platformio.ini` injects them as `-DDEFAULT_WIFI_SSID` / `-DDEFAULT_WIFI_PASS` build flags. `src/config/secrets.h.example` is the template for the header-based variant.

## Build and Flash

### Requirements

- [VS Code](https://code.visualstudio.com/) with the [PlatformIO](https://platformio.org/install/ide?install=vscode) extension
- Or [PlatformIO Core](https://platformio.org/install/cli) via command line

### Compile and Upload

```bash
# Compile the project
pio run

# Upload firmware to ESP32
pio run --target upload

# Monitor serial port
pio device monitor

# Clean build files
pio run --target clean
```

### Upload Web Interface Files

The HTML, CSS, JS, fonts and images are in the `data/` folder and are stored in the ESP32's **LittleFS** partition. After modifying any web file:

```bash
pio run --target uploadfs
```

### Debug Configuration

In `src/config/debug_config.h` you control what appears on the serial port. One verbosity level plus one switch per subsystem:

| Macro | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| `DEBUGLEVEL_NONE` (0) | Nothing |
| `DEBUGLEVEL_ERRORS` (1) | `debugE` / `debuglnE` |
| `DEBUGLEVEL_WARNINGS` (2) | Errors + `debugW` / `debuglnW` |
| `DEBUGLEVEL_DEBUGGING` (3) | Warnings + `debugD` / `debuglnD` — current default |
| `DEBUGLEVEL_VERBOSE` (4) | Everything, including `debugV` / `debuglnV` |
| `DEBUG_SYSTEM` | Init, mutexes, persistence errors |
| `DEBUG_WIFI` | WiFi connection and credentials |
| `DEBUG_WEB` | HTTP server and LittleFS |
| `DEBUG_WEBSOCKET` | WebSocket traffic and JSON payloads |
| `DEBUG_LED` | Strip, effects and brightness |
| `DEBUG_BATTERY` | Battery monitoring and `/battlog.json` |
| `DEBUG_TEMPERATURE` | DHT22 sensor |
| `DEBUG_NETWORK` | mDNS |
| `DEBUG_BLUETOOTH` | Bluetooth module and button emulation |
| `DEBUG_POWER_MANAGEMENT` | Power state machine transitions |

To build a release firmware without serial logging you do not need to edit the file — override the level from the build flags:

```bash
PLATFORMIO_BUILD_FLAGS="-DDEBUGLEVEL=0" pio run    # one-off release build
```

The persistent alternative is adding `-DDEBUGLEVEL=0` to `build_flags` in `platformio.ini`. Measured on this project: **1,085,005 B → 1,058,845 B** of flash and **58,456 B → 57,648 B** of static RAM.

### Memory & Flash

The build uses the default partition scheme (no `board_build.partitions`), so the app partition is **1,310,720 B** and LittleFS gets the remainder of the 4 MB flash:

| Metric | Used | Share |
|--------|------|---|
| RAM | 58,456 B of 327,680 B | 17.8% |
| Flash (app) | 1,085,005 B of 1,310,720 B | 82.8% |

That leaves roughly **225 KB** of app headroom — enough for new effects, not for a library that weighs hundreds of KB. The web app in `data/` (HTML, CSS, JS, three fonts, images) totals ~1.0 MB of the ~1.4 MB LittleFS partition.

Two things to know before touching versions:

- The toolchain is pinned on purpose: `platform = espressif32@7.1.3` (arduino-esp32 2.0.17) and `fastled/FastLED@3.9.20`. FastLED 3.10.x needs core 3.x / IDF 5 and does not compile here; the comments in `platformio.ini` record the sizes measured for each candidate version.
- `-DDEBUGLEVEL=0` remains the cheapest flash and RAM saving available (26 KB of flash, ~0.8 KB of RAM).

## HTTP Routes & WebSocket Protocol

Besides the static files, the server exposes a handful of endpoints:

| Route | Method | Purpose |
|-------|--------|---------|
| `/` | GET | Serves `index.html` from LittleFS |
| `/<file>` | GET | Static assets (CSS, JS, fonts, images) |
| `/wifi-info` | GET | JSON: `status`, `ssid`, `ip`, `rssi` |
| `/save-wifi` | POST | `ssid`, `password` → saved to NVS, then the device reboots |
| `/fxdata` | GET | Metadata of every effect (WLED-style strings: labels, defaults, limits) |
| `/palettes` | GET | Palette names plus six representative colors each |
| `/update` | GET | ElegantOTA upload page |
| `/ws` | WS | The real-time channel |

mDNS advertises the device as **`sml`**, so `http://sml.local` resolves as long as the phone and the lamp are on the same subnet.

### WebSocket contract

One endpoint (`/ws`), JSON text frames, up to **8 clients** — the first one connected is the master. Unknown actions are ignored.

**Client → server.** A message may carry `effectId` alone (0 = solid color, 1-50 = registry entry), which selects *and* enables that effect. Everything else travels in `action`:

| Action | Payload | Effect |
|--------|---------|--------|
| `toggle`, `power` | `state` | Strip on/off |
| `slider` | `brightness` | Strip brightness |
| `picker` | `color{r,g,b}`, optional `brightness` | Solid color |
| `lamp` | — | Main lamp relay |
| `music` | — | Bluetooth module relay |
| `animation`, `vu`, `indicator` | — | Re-render the current effect |
| `toggleBatt`, `toggleTemp` | — | Battery / temperature indicator effect |
| `randomFX` | `state`, optional `effectId` | Random FX cycling |
| `randomVU`, `randomVUConfig` | `state`, `duration`, `effectPool` | Random VU cycling |
| `randomConfig` | `mode`, `duration`, `effectPool`, `categories`, `start` | Random FX configuration |
| `setParams` | `effectId`, `speed`, `intensity`, `custom1..3`, `check1..3`, `palette` | Effect parameters |
| `setWifiTimeout` | `seconds` (5-3600) | WiFi-loss timeout |
| `requestFullState` | — | Full state, including params and metadata |
| `requestBattHistory` | — | Battery history array |
| `refreshClientList` | — | Re-send client list and action log |
| `reboot`, `factoryReset` | — | **Master only** — rejected otherwise |
| `volup`, `voldown`, `skipL`, `skipR`, `play-pause` | — | Bluetooth button emulation |
| `lv` | `bool`, optional `real` | Start/stop the Peek stream; `real` picks true colors |
| `peekColorMode` | `real` | Switch Peek color mode without restarting |

**Server → client.** A full state document — one `<effect>Status` key per effect plus `color`, `neobrightness`, the WiFi fields, `uptime`, `heap`, `ver`, `wsSlaves`, `wsMax`, `randomMode`, `effectId`, and `params`/`meta` when requested — plus lightweight sensor-only updates, `wsClientList`, `wsActionLog` (`t`, `c`, `ty`, `v1..v3`), `battHistory` (`t`, `v`, `l`), `yourClientId`, `peekBusy`, and, only for the Peek subscriber, binary frames: `0x4C`, version `0x01`, width, height `1`, then R,G,B per pixel.

## LED Effects

The project ships **47 selectable effects** organized into categories. Access them from the **Lamp** tab → Effects section. The firmware registry reserves **50 effect IDs**: three retired effects keep their slot so historical IDs stay valid.

### Categories

| Category | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| ⭐ **Favorites** | Your favorite effects (star-marked) |
| 📋 **Playlist** | Custom playlist for random mode |
| 🔧 **Fundamentals** | Base effects: solid, flash, fade |
| 🏃 **Moving** | Motion effects: dot, comet, sinelon, scan |
| ⚡ **Dynamics** | Dynamic effects: fire, lightning, aurora, popcorn |
| 🎨 **Patterns** | Patterns: rainbow, theater chase, sweep, dissolve |
| 🌡️ **States** | Status effects: battery, temperature |
| 🔀 **Random** | Random mode (see next section) |

### VU Effects (Music Visualization)

In addition to the visual effects there are **eight VU meters** that react to whatever the Bluetooth speaker is playing. They live in the **Music** tab and, like every other effect, you turn one on by tapping its card:

| VU meter | What it shows | Settings |
|----------|---------------|----------|
| **Rainbow VU** | Gradient across the strip that fills with the audio level | palette |
| **Rainbow Hue** | The same gradient with the hue cycling over time | palette |
| **Old Skool** | Classic green/red bar with a floating peak dot | none — fixed colors |
| **Ripple VU** | Ripples spreading from a random point on each beat | palette |
| **3 Bars** | Three segments that bloom and fall with the level | palette |
| **Ocean VU** | Slow waves rippling out from the center | palette |
| **Noisemeter** | Green/yellow/red scale with a white peak | Decay, Sensitivity |
| **DJ Light** | Bright pulse spreading from the center, hue shifting with the beat | Speed, Color Shift, palette |

Every VU meter except **Old Skool** and **Noisemeter** has a **gear**, and its panel is a palette list, exactly like the visual effects. All of them start on **Rainbow**, the palette they were designed around. The list is curated to the palettes that really change colour as the meter samples them — the rainbow family plus Party, Candy and C9 — so single-hue palettes (Ocean, Ice, Aurora…) and the ones that pass through black (Scanner) are not offered, because on a VU they look like one flat colour or flicker off.

### How the palettes are chosen

Every effect samples its palette in a different way, and the list it offers follows that:

- **Full sweep** (Fire, Comet, Rainbow Beat, Plasma, Pacifica, the VU meters…): the colour index moves with position, time or level, so the whole palette shows. These keep a palette list.
- **Single colour** (Sparkle, Lightning, Scanner, Heartbeat, ICU, Drip, Halloween Eyes): the render always reads the *same* index, so a palette list only ever showed one of its colours. Their panel is the **colour wheel** and they paint with the LED colour, the same one Solid uses.
- **Fixed colours**: RWB (red/white/blue), Old Skool (green/red) and Noisemeter (green/yellow/red) have no palette at all.

Every palette is generated at **full saturation**: none of them contains white, because a white step washes out the rest of the gradient and the effect stops looking like itself, and the only ones that keep a dark start are the fire ramps (they need the cold end). Palettes that look alike are never listed together: the fire ramps only appear on fire effects, and the seas and the auroras keep to their own family. The fires also come in colours — **Fire Blue**, **Fire Green** and **Fire Violet** — the same ramp with another hue; Candle offers an amber flame plus a blue and a green one; Pacifica offers seas in emerald and violet next to the classic blue; and **Amber/Blue**, **Pink/Lime** and **Violet/Yellow** are hard-edged complementary pairs for effects that want contrast instead of a rainbow. **Reset to Defaults**, at the bottom of every panel, restores the sliders and the default palette.

![VU Effect](https://github.com/alexminator/SML/blob/master/img/Vu%20effect.png)

### How to Activate, Turn Off and Configure an Effect

The effect cards work as an **on/off switch**, and the settings live behind a separate **⚙ gear** button.

| Action | Desktop | Mobile |
|--------|---------|--------|
| **Turn the effect on** | Click the card | Tap the card |
| **Turn the effect off** | Click the card again (the active one) | Tap the card again (the active one) |
| **Open the effect settings** | Click the **⚙ gear** in the card's top-left corner, or the gear in the **Peek** panel | Tap the **⚙ gear** on the card, or **long-press** the card (~0.5 s) |

Tapping the active card again **turns the strip off** — there is no fallback effect. To keep a plain color instead of an effect, use the **Solid color** card at the top of the **Lamp** tab: pick the color with the wheel and set the brightness with the slider.

The configuration panel lets you adjust:

- **Speed** — effect speed
- **Color** — palette or primary color
- **Intensity** — brightness or effect intensity
- Effect-specific parameters

The **Random FX** and **Random VU** cards behave the same way: tap/click to start or stop the cycling, gear (or long-press) to open the cycle settings.

Any client can turn effects on and off, but only the **master** client can open the settings panel (see the *Master / Slave Mode* section below). The strip has to be on for the cards to respond.

## Power Management

The ESP32 implements an automatic power management system with a state machine that detects the power source and optimizes consumption.

### States

| State | Power Source | WiFi | CPU | Neopixel | Consumption |
|--------|--------|------|-----|----------|---------|
| **AC_MODE** | Mains | Always ON | 240 MHz | On | ~180 mA |
| **BATTERY_CONNECTING** | Battery | 10s ON / 30s wait | 240 MHz | Off | ~120 mA |
| **BATTERY_ACTIVE** | Battery + client connected | Always ON | 240 MHz | Off | ~120 mA |
| **BATTERY_SLEEP** | Battery, no client | 10s ON / 60s OFF | 80 MHz | Off | ~25 mA |

### Transitions
- **AC loss** → try to connect 10s → wait for client 30s → sleep if no client
- **Client connects** → instant wake from any state
- **Client disconnects** → wait 30s for reconnection → sleep
- **AC restored** → immediate return to full operation
- **Battery < 15%** → force maximum power saving

### Power Source Detection
Uses the TP4056 pins: if charging or battery is full, it assumes AC power is present. Includes 3-second debounce to prevent rapid state changes from power flickers.

### What Happens During a Power Outage
When mains power is lost, the load-sharing circuit automatically disconnects the battery from the mains path and the ESP32 runs from the battery through the DC-DC boost converter. In that situation:

- The **Neopixel strip turns off** because it is powered only from the mains supply and not from the battery (the Neopixels consume too much for battery operation).
- The **main LED lamp, the Bluetooth speaker, and the ESP32 itself keep running** on battery power, so music continues playing and the web interface remains controllable.
- The device automatically enters battery mode: first it tries to reconnect WiFi for 10 s, then waits up to 30 s for a WebSocket client to connect, and if nobody connects it falls back to power-saving cycles (WiFi 10 s on / 60 s off, CPU at 80 MHz).
- Consumption in battery drops from ~120 mA (battery + connected client) to ~25 mA (battery, no client).

### What Happens When the Battery Is Low / Discharging
- Below 15% the state machine forces maximum power savings.
- The voltage history is automatically saved to **/battlog.json** every N readings and at least once per minute, so when mains returns you can see how the battery behaved during the outage.

### What Happens When WiFi Is Lost
There are two situations that are not the same:

1. **The device loses the WiFi link (the ESP32 loses WiFi).** This happens if the router goes down, the device goes out of range, or the router reboots. The ESP32 reports `wifiOk:false` to all clients, and if you are in AC mode the **master** client sees a countdown on the web interface before the strip is turned off. The strip does not turn off immediately; it waits for the configured WiFi-loss timeout (default 30 s, configurable in the Config tab) and only turns off if the link is not restored.
2. **A client disconnects (for example, your phone goes to sleep or you leave the network).** If the device still has WiFi, the strip **does NOT turn off**, because the ESP32 is still connected. Only the clients that are actually connected to the device see and control the strip.

### Master / Slave Mode (Multi-Device Control)
The web interface supports up to **8 simultaneous clients** connected to the same ESP32 at once, but there is one important concept: among all those clients there is only **one master**.

- The **first device that connects** becomes the master.
- The master is the only one that can turn the strip off when mains power is lost (the WiFi-loss countdown is shown only to the master).
- All clients see changes in real time (color, effect, brightness, music, battery), but the strip's critical power-level actions (turn off on mains loss, turn off on WiFi-loss timeout) are controlled only by the master.
- When the master disconnects, the **next client in the list becomes master automatically** (handover). A new client does not need to be "chosen": the ESP32 decides.

### What You CAN Do From a Slave Device
- Turn the main LED lamp on/off.
- Turn the Bluetooth speaker on/off and control it (play/pause, skip forward/backward, volume).
- Change the strip color, brightness, and effect (the master reflects these too).
- Activate/deactivate the status effects (battery, temperature).
- View all real-time state: battery, temperature, WiFi, connected clients, activity log.
- View the client list and who is master.

### What You CANNOT Do From a Slave Device
- Turn the strip off on mains loss or on WiFi-loss timeout: that decision is made by the master. If you are a slave and mains is lost, the strip turns off because the master orders it (or because the device switches to battery and the strip no longer has 5V).
- Turn the strip off if you are a slave and the ESP32 still has WiFi: the strip keeps running because the master is still controlling it.
- Force a reboot or a factory reset: those actions are reserved for the master.
- See the WiFi-loss countdown banner: that banner appears only on the master.

In practice this means several family members can control the lamp at the same time without conflicting, but the "turn off on power loss" or "turn off on WiFi loss" behavior is always decided by whichever device is currently the master.

## Data & Persistence

Everything the device remembers lives in flash, so it survives a power cut:

| Stored where | File / namespace | Contents |
|--------------|------------------|----------|
| LittleFS | `/state.json` | Color, brightness, `effectId`, power state, WiFi-loss timeout, Random mode configuration |
| LittleFS | `/params.json` | Per-effect parameters (speed, intensity, custom1-3, checks, palette). Writes are debounced, so dragging a slider does not wear the flash |
| LittleFS | `/battlog.json` | Battery history: 200-entry circular buffer, one entry per ≥0.10 V change or every 60 s, flushed every 5 entries |
| NVS | `Preferences` namespace `wifi` | SSID and password saved from the Config tab |

Some preferences never reach the ESP32 — they belong to the browser (`localStorage`): favorites, the custom playlist, Random duration/mode and the theme. They do not follow you to another device, and clearing the site data resets them.

A **factory reset** (master only, Config tab) erases the WiFi credentials, `/params.json`, `/state.json` and `/battlog.json`, then reboots into the compiled defaults.

## Troubleshooting

- **`sml.local` does not resolve.** mDNS needs both devices on the same subnet. Use the IP shown in Config → System Info.
- **A card does nothing.** Either the strip is off (one tap turns it on), or you are a slave opening a settings panel only the master may edit, or another client changed the state a second later — every client shares one lamp.
- **The strip turned itself off.** The WiFi link was lost for longer than the configured timeout (Config → WiFi loss protection); it resumes when the link returns. A client disconnecting on its own does *not* trigger this.
- **Peek reports busy.** Only one device can stream the preview at a time; stop it on the other device first.
- **The web UI looks stale.** Reload the page — a service worker caches the assets, so a hard reload or clearing the site data pulls the new ones.
- **Upload fails.** Check the serial port configured in `platformio.ini` (or pass `--upload-port`), and keep `upload_speed = 921600` only if your board tolerates it.
- **`pio run` complains about the build directory.** After an interrupted build, delete `.pio/build` and run it again.
